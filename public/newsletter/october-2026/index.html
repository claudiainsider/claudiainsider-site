This Month's Numbers

September Came Back Loud

After a sleepy August, San Francisco's fall market showed up with multiple offers, short timelines, and a condo rebound nobody was calling a year ago.

The median single-family home sold for $2.058 million in September, up 18.6% from a year ago, and it took a median of just 12 days to go into contract. Condos did something I want you to notice: the median condo price hit $1.325 million, up 19.6% year-over-year, and condos went into contract in 14 days. Last September that number was 26.

Inventory did its usual post-summer rebound. Single-family listings rose roughly 40% from August to 235 homes, and condos climbed to 441 units, but we're still about 32% below last year's supply. Fewer homes, more buyers, faster decisions. That's the whole month in one sentence.

76% of SF homes and condos sold above asking in September. A year ago, that number was 54%.

The competition number is the one everyone will quote: single-family homes sold for a median of nearly 29% above asking, and condos about 10% above. Both are three-year highs. Before you panic, or celebrate, depending on which side you're on, I need you to read the Money Lesson below, because that 29% is doing a lot less work than it looks like it's doing.

The Charts

What the Data Says

SF Median Sale Price — Single-Family Homes

What it means: The spring run-up to $2.15M cooled over the summer, and September bounced back to $2.058M. Prices aren't climbing the way they did in March, but they're holding about 18% above where they were a year ago. The floor keeps moving up.

SF Median Sale Price — Condos

What it means: Condos were the story of September. $1.325M is nearly back to the April peak and almost 20% above last year. If you've been waiting for the condo market to "come back" before selling, this is what that looks like.

Sale Price as % of Asking — SFH vs. Condos

What it means: Single-family homes averaged 129% of asking in September and condos 110%, both three-year highs. Part of that is demand. Part of it is how SF agents price, and I get into that below. Either way, "list price" and "sale price" have never been further apart.

Months of Supply — SFH vs. Condos

What it means: Three months of supply is the usual line for a balanced market. SFH sits at 1.2 and condos at 2.0, down from 3.5 last September. The condo side has gone from buyer-friendly to competitive in under a year.

Active Inventory — SFH vs. Condos

What it means: September brought the seasonal wave of new listings, 235 single-family homes and 441 condos, but it's still 25% fewer houses and 34% fewer condos than a year ago. Homeowners sitting on sub-4% mortgages aren't in a hurry to move, and SF isn't building its way out of it.

30-Year Fixed Mortgage Rate — Weekly

What it means: Rates went from 6.66% to 7.40% in seven weeks, the fastest move of the year. On a $1.2M loan, that's roughly $600 more per month. And yet SF kept overbidding, which tells you a lot about who's buying here right now.

National Backdrop

The Bigger Picture

Most of the country is cooling. SF is not. Here's the context for why those two things can both be true.

Mortgage Rates

The 30-year fixed hit 7.40% on October 15, up from 6.27% a year ago. The Fed meets October 27–28 and markets put an 85% chance on no change, but with inflation running hot, more than half of investors now expect two additional hikes by year-end. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury, not the Fed's press conference, so don't plan your purchase around a meeting date.

Inflation & Energy

CPI rose to 3.7% in September, up from 3.4% in August. Energy did the damage: fuel oil up 74.2%, gasoline up 34.2%, airfare up 22.4%. Shelter costs rose a comparatively calm 3.2%. The part that matters for you: as long as that headline number keeps rising, rate relief stays on hold.

Cash Is King

31.4% of U.S. home sales in the first four months of 2026 were all-cash, with California at 24%. Cash shows up at both ends: 67% of homes under $100K and 57% of homes between $5M and $10M. This is a big piece of why SF keeps overbidding while rates climb. A meaningful slice of our buyers aren't borrowing at all.

The Lock-In

Nearly 78% of American mortgage holders have a rate below 6%, almost half are below 4%, and in California 27% are below 3%. If you own one of those, you already know why you're not selling. Multiply that by every homeowner in the city, and that's our inventory problem.

The Money Lesson

Why "29% Over Asking" Isn't 29% Appreciation

Every fall someone sends me a headline about SF homes selling for 30% over asking and asks if they've already missed the boat. So let's talk about what that number actually measures, because it's one of the most misread stats in SF real estate.

In most of the country, the list price is the seller's opening ask, and the sale price lands somewhere below it after negotiation. In San Francisco, we do it backwards. That's strategic underpricing: the list price is set deliberately below what the seller expects, to pull in more buyers, create competition, and let the market set the number on offer day. The "29% over asking" isn't 29% of unexpected appreciation. A good chunk of it was baked into the list price on purpose.

I'm seeing this play out in real time. I'm currently working with a buyer who bought their last home in 2008, and it has been a learning lesson for them, because the way real estate operated back then versus now is completely different. In 2008 you could offer under asking and expect a conversation. Today, the asking price is usually not what the seller is actually looking for, unless the home is transparently priced or sitting pretty on the nose of what comps are trading for in the area. Unlearning that is half the work of buying in SF right now.

How to read a listing in this market

1. Treat the list price as a marketing number. It tells you where the seller wants the bidding to start, not where they expect it to end. The real number lives in the comps: what similar homes on similar blocks actually closed for in the last 60 to 90 days.

2. "Over asking" is a stat about the list price, not the house. Two homes close at $1.5M. One was listed at $1.2M and sold 25% over, the other at $1.45M and sold 3% over. Same house, same money, totally different headline. If you're using the overbid to judge how hot a home was, you're really just measuring how low the agent set the opener. Look at what it closed for and skip the drama.

3. Ask your agent for the expected range before you fall in love. Any decent agent, hi, can tell you within a few percent where a listing is likely to land by reading the comps and talking to the listing agent. Walk into the open house already knowing that number, and the list price stops messing with your head. That's how you win without overpaying: you bid with conviction up to the number the comps support, adjusted for how badly you want this particular house and the features that make it unlike any other. Every single time, the buyers competing for a home value its quirks differently. The corner lot, the extra bedroom, the garden you'd actually use. Nobody can assess that for you. You have to decide, before offer day, what this specific home is worth to you, and let it go when the bidding runs past that number. The buyers who lose in this market aren't the ones who got outbid. They're the ones who paid for the crowd instead of the house.

If you're selling

Underpricing only works if the home is ready for the crowd it invites. The three Vantage sales above weren't just priced well, they were prepped, staged, photographed, and launched on a schedule. Price low on a home that isn't ready and you'll get the crowd without the offers. That's the version of this strategy that keeps me up at night.

Worth Knowing

Two Things on the SF Calendar

Neighborhood schools are coming back

On October 13 the Board of Education voted 5–2 to move toward replacing SF's elementary school lottery with neighborhood-based assignment, likely starting with the 2028–29 school year. Boundaries aren't drawn yet. In most cities, living near a sought-after public school carries a price premium. SF's lottery mostly broke that link, and this vote could put it back. If you're buying with a long horizon, it's worth knowing which elementary school is closest, even if kids aren't in the plan yet.

November 3 ballot: the housing measures

Prop C expands affordable-housing funding and extends the Housing Trust Fund through 2058. Prop H is a parcel tax to fund Muni; the Controller's Office estimates that if it fails and service cuts follow, home values could dip 0.6% to 0.9%, roughly $9,400 to $14,100 on an average home. Prop I dedicates transfer-tax revenue from $10M+ sales to affordable housing. Prop J removes certain transfer-tax exemptions on foreclosures. I'm not here to tell you how to vote, but these four touch your home's value more directly than anything at the top of the ballot.

Insider Spot

Foghorn Taproom

846 Divisadero St · NoPa · Wings, Beer & Arcade

I have eaten a lot of lemon pepper wings in my life, and the ones at Foghorn on Divisadero are the best I've had. Full stop. All flats, extra lemon pepper sauce on the side, because I like my wings extra tangy and that sauce belongs on the fries too. Foghorn is a casual sports bar with a long beer list and a pizza menu, but I'll be honest: I'm there for the chicken. There are a few Foghorn locations around the city. Divisadero is the only one I've been to, so that's the one I can vouch for.

What makes it an Insider pick is that it works for every version of a night out. It's the kind of place you'd post up for a Valkyries game or March Madness. If sports aren't your thing, there's a small arcade in the back, and one of the cabinets has over 500 retro games on a single console. My boyfriend and I play Street Fighter on it. I don't drink, but every friend of mine who does swears by the beer selection, especially the sours. And it's a great walking spot: when you're done, Emporium is right up the street for more games. The neighborhood favorite is Wing Wednesday Power Hour: 50-cent wings from 5 to 6 pm with a drink, sets of five, up to 10 wings per drink, dine-in only. It gets packed, so check their Instagram before you go.

What I'd Order

Lemon pepper wings, all flats, extra sauce on the side. Mango habanero wings if you can handle heat. Seasoned fries, or the loaded fries with a reaper tender on top. Brussels sprouts and the fried chicken sandwich round it out.
